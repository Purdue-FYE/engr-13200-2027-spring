5.3.1. MA: Data Manipulation and Visualization#

Background#

Mechanical vibrations occur in many engineering systems, including vehicles, manufacturing equipment, buildings, and spacecraft. Engineers study these vibrations to predict how systems respond to disturbances and evaluate whether they can operate safely and effectively.

For example, NASA performs vibration testing on spacecraft to evaluate whether they can withstand the forces experienced during launch (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2023).

Modeling Mechanical Vibrations#

One way engineers study mechanical vibrations is through a mass–spring–damper system. This simplified system represents how an object responds to applied forces and how its motion changes over time (Massachusetts Institute of Technology [MIT], 2011).

The mass–spring–damper system shown in Fig. 5.9 includes the following components and variables:

  • Spring constant (\(k\)): Describes the stiffness of the spring and its resistance to deformation.

  • Damping coefficient (\(B\)): Describes the resistance provided by the damper as the mass moves.

  • Applied force (\(F\)): The external force acting on the system.

  • Mass (\(m\)): The mass of the object whose motion is being studied.

  • Displacement (\(x\)): The horizontal position of the mass relative to its equilibrium position.

When the mass is displaced from its equilibrium position and released, the spring produces a restoring force that causes the mass to oscillate. The damper dissipates energy, gradually reducing the amplitude of the oscillations. After the mass is released, the applied external force is assumed to be zero.

The displacement, in millimeters (mm), of an underdamped system can be represented using the following simplified mathematical model:

(5.1)#\[x(t) = Ae^{-bt}\cos(2\pi ft)\]

The model describes how the system’s displacement changes over time. The cosine function represents the oscillating motion, while the exponential term represents the decrease in amplitude due to damping.

Damping coefficient (\(B\)) vs exponential decay constant (\(b\))

The damping coefficient (\(B\)) in Fig. 5.9 describes a physical property of the damper, while the exponential decay constant (\(b\)) in Equation (5.1) describes how quickly the oscillation amplitude decreases. These parameters are related but are not interchangeable.

An engineering team is evaluating the vibration response of a mechanical system. Three simulated experimental trials have been provided, representing repeated displacement measurements over time.

Task 1: MATLAB Script#

Programming Standards

Review and reference the MATLAB Programming Standards Guide to ensure your code follows the required formatting and documentation standards, including the use of clear and descriptive comments.

Part 1: Mathematical Model#

  1. Define variables for the initial displacement amplitude, exponential decay constant, and oscillation frequency using the values provided in Table 5.5.

  2. Create a time vector containing 201 evenly spaced values from 0 to 10 seconds.

  3. Use the mathematical model provided in Equation (5.1) to calculate the predicted displacement at each time value. Perform the calculations using array operations without loops.

Part 2: Experimental Data Analysis#

  1. Open the provided vibration_test_data.csv file and examine how the measurements are structured.

  2. Import the CSV file into MATLAB. Use array indexing to extract and organize the time values and displacement measurements needed for your analysis.

  3. One experimental measurement is missing and is represented by NaN in the dataset. Identify the missing measurement and replace it with the average of the measurements immediately before and after it in the same trial. Make this replacement in MATLAB without modifying the original CSV file.

  4. Calculate the mean displacement across the three trials at each recorded time. Your results should contain one mean displacement value for each experimental time value.

Part 3: Data Visualization#

  1. Create one figure comparing the modeled displacement with the experimental results.

    The figure must include:

    • A smooth, continuous line or curve representing the mathematical model.

    • Individual markers representing the mean experimental displacement at each recorded time value.

    • A descriptive title.

    • Axis labels identifying the variables and their units.

    • A legend identifying the mathematical model and experimental mean.

    • Grid lines.

    Display the model and experimental results on the same set of axes.

  2. Save the completed figure as username_5_3_1.png.

Part 4: Results Display#

  1. Use array indexing to determine the modeled displacement and mean experimental displacement values at 5 seconds.

  2. Calculate the absolute difference between these two displacement values.

  3. Use fprintf to display the modeled displacement, mean experimental displacement, and absolute difference at 5 seconds. Report all values with appropriate units and precision.
